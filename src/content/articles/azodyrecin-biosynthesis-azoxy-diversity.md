The azoxy group — RN=N⁺(O⁻)R — is one of the strangest functional groups in natural product chemistry: a polar N-oxide on a nitrogen–nitrogen double bond, stable enough to survive extraction and chromatography, yet one reductive activation away from a DNA-alkylating toxin. Synthetic chemists have known it for over a century; biology seemed to use it almost nowhere. Seventy years of isolation work — from elaiomycin in 1954 onward — have been quietly correcting that impression, one eccentric structure at a time. In this 2024 *ACS Chemical Biology* paper, Maleckis, Wibowo, and colleagues dissect the biosynthesis of the cytotoxic azodyrecins from the soil isolate *Streptomyces mirabilis* P8-A2. The paper rewards a close reading twice over: once as pathway enzymology, and once — the emphasis here — as a portrait of a structurally diverse and plainly undercounted corner of nature's chemistry.

## The paper at a glance

The authors sequenced the producer's genome, delineated the azodyrecin (*azd*) cluster, and confirmed it by heterologous expression in *S. coelicolor* M1146 and *S. albidoflavus* J1074. Fourteen genes were then knocked out in the native producer using CRISPR-BEST base editing, and untargeted LC-MS/MS metabolomics of the mutant panel, organized by feature-based molecular networking, yielded eleven proposed intermediates. Among them were the prizes of the study: hydrazine and azo compounds, the long-postulated precursors of the azoxy group. Finally, all known azoxy cluster types were mapped onto a phylogenomic tree of 1528 *Streptomyces* genomes to ask how common this chemistry actually is. The answer — about one genome in five carries an azoxy cluster — is the finding this perspective wants in front of everyone who screens actinomycetes.

## The chemical diversity behind a single functional group

Natural azoxy compounds are best understood not as a family but as a *bond* that evolution has installed into otherwise unrelated architectures. Valanimycin couples two amino acid-derived pieces; azoxybacilin, an antifungal from *Bacillus cereus*, shows the motif is not even restricted to actinomycetes. A larger group couples a fatty amine to an amino acid: elaiomycin, the antifungal KA57A, the maniwamycins, the nematocidal jietacins, and the cytotoxic azodyrecins A–F. The azoxymycins abandon pairing altogether, arising by dimerization of a phenolic monomer through an enzymatic aniline-to-nitroso oxidation. And beyond bacteria, cycads accumulate azoxyglycosides such as cycasin: this functional group has been invented more than once.

The group's chemical personality explains much of this natural history. The polar N-oxide carries the characteristic UV chromophore, yet lipophilic substituents let the metabolites behave on silica like ordinary fatty natural products. E/Z isomerism about the N=N bond historically made the group easy to misread — elaiomycin's structure was settled in 1956 only through chemical degradation. Under reducing conditions the azoxy group steps back down the same oxidation ladder the pathway climbs up, and those reduced forms account for much of the family's reputation for toxicity and mutagenicity. A reasonable reading of the bioactivity pattern — inference, not proof — is that the azoxy group is the reactive pharmacophore while the flanking subunits tune delivery, potency, and spectrum: nature's combinatorial experiment has been run on the decorations while the warhead stays constant.

There is even diversity *within* single producers, and it is biosynthetically eloquent: the elaiomycin strain also yields the alkylhydrazides elaiomycins B and C, formally reduced relatives of the azoxy products. Hydrazine-level congeners co-isolated with azoxy products are exactly what a hydrazine → azo → azoxy oxidative sequence predicts — and the genetic evidence for that sequence is precisely what this paper supplies.

## How nature builds the azoxy bond

The mechanistic heart of the paper is the fate of four conserved genes — *azdA*, *azdB*, *azdH*, *azdO* — that recur across the known azoxy cluster types. Knockout metabolomics assigns them a working division of labor. AzdH, with its partner AzdR, is proposed to N-hydroxylate the fatty amine; when *azdH* is inactivated, the amine is captured instead as an amide shunt product. AzdA, an aminoacyl-tRNA-dependent transferase, is proposed to deliver alanine onto the hydroxylated amine, setting up the N–N connectivity through a hydrazine intermediate. The decisive observation concerns AzdB: its knockout accumulates hydrazine and azo compounds but no azoxy products, identifying AzdB as the azo-to-azoxy oxidase. The overall sequence — amine, hydroxylamine, hydrazine, azo, azoxy — turns a seventy-year-old structural mystery into an ordered oxidation ladder, and it dovetails with the 2023 in vitro reconstitution of the homologous valanimycin cascade. Tailoring is equally legible: AzdM methylates the carboxylate, and the desaturase AzdT installs the double bond last, converting azodyrecins D–F into A–C.

## A genus-wide census

The paper's most quotable numbers come last. Across 1528 *Streptomyces* genomes, 351 — about 22% — carry a recognizable azoxy cluster: azodyrecin-like in 191 genomes, KA57A-like in 136, valanimycin- and azoxymycin-like rare. Even *S. coelicolor* A3(2), the most studied streptomycete in existence, harbors an azodyrecin-like cluster yet has never yielded an azoxy compound: the chemical record and the genomic record are badly out of register. The authors converted their cluster definitions into antiSMASH detection rules, so the census is repeatable by anyone with a genome. For a field that has described perhaps a dozen natural azoxy families in seventy years, a one-in-five carriage rate is not a footnote; it is a discovery forecast.

## Limitations and open questions

A close reading should hold onto what the paper does not establish:

- **The intermediates are proposals, not isolates.** Compounds 7–17 are annotated from exact mass and MS/MS in mutant extracts, not from purified material with NMR support — one layer below structural proof.
- **The N–N bond-forming step is unassigned.** AzdO is the leading candidate, but its knockout gave no detectable intermediates, and which steps are enzymatic versus spontaneous is unresolved.
- **No in vitro chemistry here.** Every activity is inferred from knockout phenotypes in the native producer; AzdB's cofactor requirements and mechanism remain to be demonstrated.
- **Precursors are read from structures, not labels.** The starter units are inferred from products and gene annotations; isotope feeding would firm this up.
- **Cluster presence is not production.** The census cannot say which of the 351 clusters are expressed; silent clusters may encode azodyrecin — or something azoxy but new.

## What to take from it

For a practicing natural product chemist, three lessons transfer. First, put the azoxy group in your search image: a functional group encoded in a fifth of *Streptomyces* genomes but isolated perhaps a dozen times is exactly the blind spot that reactivity-based screening and targeted MS/MS features can exploit. Second, read reduced congeners as pathway echoes: hydrazides or hydrazines co-occurring with oxidized N–N products are not noise but breadcrumbs marking the oxidative ladder. Third, the study is a model of how to dissect a bond-forming pathway without in vitro biochemistry: base-editing knockouts plus network-organized metabolomics caught even transient intermediates, because the analysis compared mutant and wild-type extracts feature by feature. The deepest lesson is the one this publication exists to examine: a functional group can be genetically common and chemically invisible at the same time. Somewhere in the extracts already sitting in freezers, the other three hundred clusters are waiting to be noticed.
